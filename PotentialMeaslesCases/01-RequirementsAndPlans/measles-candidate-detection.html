Unreported Potential Measles Cases

One way SHIN-NY might help: scan TRN, ADT, CCD, and lab data for hints of possible measles that were never reported as a reportable condition — surfacing candidates for public-health follow-up, not diagnoses.

← Back to Measles Response Overview
Status: early idea, framework only. This sketches the question and a first-pass approach so a developer can pick it up. The signal definitions below are starting points, meant to be refined as we see what the data actually holds.

The Question

New York mandates reporting of measles, so confirmed, reported cases are already handled — those are not what we're looking for. We're interested in the opposite: patients whose data carries a hint of possible measles that was never reported as the reportable condition, and that might someday turn out to be measles.

The idea is to combine signals across all four data types — TRN, ADT, CCD, and lab results (ORU) — to surface candidates for public-health review. No single source is complete, and the interesting cases are the ones that slip through precisely because they don't look obvious in any one feed.

This is not a diagnosis. The output is a candidate list for public-health follow-up. Whether any candidate is actually measles is for clinicians and the health department to determine.

Why This Is a SHIN-NY-Shaped Problem — Scoped Realistically

PDR centralizes CCDs from over 2,000 contributing sources — roughly 200 hospitals and 1,000 lab sources, plus thousands of non-hospital practices (primary care, pediatrics, urgent care, and more) that no single hospital or lab system ever sees on its own.

We don't have everyone's data, and we won't catch every potential case. This is not a comprehensive surveillance system, and it should never be described as one. The claim is more modest: because we see across thousands of non-hospital sources that individually have no visibility into each other, we think we can surface a non-zero number of candidates earlier than they would otherwise be found — potentially before a positive diagnosis is reached anywhere in the system.

The value proposition in one sentence: not "we will catch every measles case," but "we can catch some cases earlier than anyone else currently can, because of where we sit in the data." That's the claim this exploration needs to prove or disprove.

The Basic Explore Loop

1
Decide on a sample methodology. For this exploration the answer is straightforward: scan all sources, statewide, but only a recent window — the last few days. PDR holds years of history, but there's no value in looking back over it here: we're trying to catch cases that haven't been reported yet, and an old unreported hint is either long since resolved or long since stale. Breadth across sources matters far more than depth across time.
2
Decide what to look for. Define a "potential measles" hint in each data type — the codes, lab orders, and encounter patterns that count.
3
Validate the signals against known, reported cases. Before trusting a signal definition on unreported data, run it against patients who were confirmed and reported as measles. If the signals don't reliably light up on cases we already know were measles, the definitions aren't ready to be trusted on cases we don't know about yet. This known-case population is the best training and tuning signal available for this problem.
4
See what we find. Run the validated signals against the recent, unreported population, remove anything already reported, and review the remaining candidates.
Loop steps 2 through 4 — tightening the signal definitions and re-validating against known cases — until the candidate list feels trustworthy (few obvious false positives, no obvious misses).
AI helps design it; deterministic code runs it. AI may be used to help explore the data and propose or tune signal definitions during steps 2–3. What actually runs against patient data is deterministic, rule-based code — not a model making live judgment calls. That code goes through the normal software lifecycle like anything else: human review, a code security scan, and a full release process, before it ever touches real data.

What a "Potential Measles" Hint Might Look Like (First Pass)

Starting points per data type, not a finished code set. Expect to add, remove, and reweight.

Data TypeWhere to LookExample Signals
Lab results (ORU) Result observations (OBX) Measles IgM / IgG serology, measles PCR / RNA orders or results; rubeola testing
CCD — Problems / Encounters Problem list, encounter diagnoses Measles ICD-10 (B05.x); SNOMED measles findings; febrile rash illness, "rule out measles," viral exanthem
ADT Admit/visit reason, diagnosis segments ED or inpatient visits for fever + rash; isolation/airborne-precaution flags; pediatric febrile rash presentations
TRN Discharge summaries / transition notes Narrative mentions of measles, rubeola, Koplik spots, maculopapular rash with fever and coryza/conjunctivitis
Clinical shorthand to consider: classic measles is fever plus the "three C's" — cough, coryza, conjunctivitis — followed by a maculopapular rash, sometimes with Koplik spots. A combination of these across sources is a stronger hint than any single code.

Excluding Already-Reported Cases (Future Feature)

Because measles is reportable, a confirmed case already flows to public health through the mandated channel. Ideally, those cases should never show up on this candidate list at all — surfacing a case the team already knows about just adds noise and erodes trust in the tool.

For the initial version, there is no automated exclusion. Candidates are surfaced purely on signal strength, and a reviewer may see cases that are already known and being handled.

Future feature idea: have the state's reportable-condition systems — both the upstate and downstate (NYC) systems — periodically send SHIN-NY a list of already-reported measles cases. We match that list against our candidates and remove any match before the list reaches a reviewer. This keeps the team from being overwhelmed by cases that are already being worked, without requiring us to solve report-matching logic on our own.

This is deliberately a phase-2 idea, not a blocker for a first version. A first version can ship, prove the candidate signals are useful, and add this exclusion feed once there's a reviewer workflow worth protecting from noise.

Open Questions (Help Wanted)

  • Legal basis: the health department has clear statutory authority over reported measles cases. Does that authority extend to likely or candidate cases that have not been reported or confirmed? This determines what the department can lawfully receive, and what SHIN-NY can lawfully send, before any real data moves — it needs legal and Privacy & Security review, not an assumption.
  • Exactly how many recent days is the right window — and does that need to flex during an active outbreak vs. steady-state monitoring?
  • Which measles LOINC / SNOMED / ICD-10 codes actually appear in our feeds versus the ones we assume?
  • How do we combine weak signals across TRN, ADT, CCD, and lab into a single candidate score?
  • How many known/reported cases are available to validate against, and is that enough to trust the signal definitions?
  • What's an acceptable false-positive rate for a candidate list meant for public-health follow-up, given that already-reported cases won't be filtered out in v1?
  • Who would own the relationship with upstate/downstate reportable-condition systems to make the future exclusion feed possible?
  • Who receives the candidate list, and in what form, so it's actionable without over-alerting?
The goal of this document is to facilitate a rich dialogue, not to present a firm, fixed idea. We've found that starting from something concrete — even an early, imperfect sketch — fosters a better conversation than starting from a blank sheet.
