42 CFR Part 2 — Candidate Identification

Building a research list for our Qualified Entities: sources whose CCD data shows patterns consistent with substance use disorder treatment, so the QE can look into whether the data needs the confidentiality protections 42 CFR Part 2 requires.

← Back to Explore

The Question We're Answering

To ensure the SHIN-NY is not releasing 42 CFR Part 2-protected data in error, we ask a simple question of the data submitted to the standard (non-Part-2) pipeline: are there sources whose clinical content shows indicators of being a Part 2 substance use disorder treatment program, even though their data was submitted as standard?

When we find one, we hand it to the responsible Qualified Entity with a clear message: we are not saying this source is, or is not, Part 2 — we are asking you to look. The QE contacts the source, does the legwork, and makes the actual determination. If a source turns out to be Part 2 but was routed as standard, the QE engages their privacy and security team so we can build a specific remediation plan together.

This is imperfect by nature — you can't definitively prove Part 2 status from clinical content alone. So the tool produces a prioritized, evidence-backed research list, not a verdict.

Why This Matters

  • Compliance exposure: Part 2 data sitting in the general bucket is queryable without the extra protections federal law requires, regardless of whether anyone has actually queried it yet.
  • Patient trust: Part 2 exists because substance use patients face real stigma. A misrouted source undermines the consent framework those patients were told they could rely on.
  • We can't fix what we don't know about: the goal here is visibility — giving QEs a prioritized, evidence-backed list of sources to look into, rather than waiting for an audit to surface the issue.
  • A low bar for inclusion is the right call: a source that turns out to be fine after a QE looks into it costs us a conversation. A source we never flag because our bar was too high costs us a compliance gap we don't know exists. We'd rather ask a few extra questions than miss the real ones.

What We Look For: The Clinical Signature of an SUD Treatment Facility

A facility whose primary business is treating substance use disorder produces clinical documents that look measurably different from a general medical practice's. Looking across a source's CCDs in aggregate, a treatment program leaves a distinctive, repeating footprint. The code rates each CCD for a high concentration of these signals:

Each CCD is scored on a 0–100 point scale. Every signal category contributes up to a maximum number of points, and the weights reflect how strongly each category points to a Part 2 treatment facility:

SignalMax PointsWhere we find itWhy this weight
SUD diagnoses 25 ICD-10 F10–F19 (alcohol, opioid, cocaine, polysubstance, etc.), excluding F17 nicotine Core clinical indicator — the reason for care
OTP / SUD billing & procedure codes 25 H0020, S0109, H0015, G2067–G2078, UDS, SBIRT, counseling Used almost exclusively by treatment programs
Treatment-model encounters 25 Detox, intensive outpatient (IOP), residential, recurring OTP visits Describe a care model, not a one-off event
MAT medications 20 Methadone, buprenorphine/suboxone, naltrexone Methadone is nearly diagnostic (OTP-only for OUD); buprenorphine is weaker alone (used in office practice too)
Facility name 5 Custodian name containing "recovery", "addiction", "methadone", etc. Minor corroboration — a hint, never decisive on its own
Total possible 100
Source score is a weighted average. After scoring each CCD 0–100, we roll the sample up to a single source score using a weighted average that gives more influence to CCDs carrying strong, unambiguous signals (methadone, OTP codes). This keeps a handful of clearly-Part-2 documents from being washed out by a larger volume of routine records, while preventing a single outlier from dominating. The QE letter shows the per-category point breakdown so the QE can see exactly which signals drove the flag.
We only count activity delivered at the encounter itself. A patient mentioning during an ER visit that they take methadone from a clinic elsewhere is patient-reported history, not evidence that the ER is a treatment program — and we exclude it. What moves a source onto the list is a sustained, population-level pattern of the facility actively providing SUD treatment. We also look at the specific care location within a source, since a hospital can contain a dedicated SUD unit even when the hospital overall looks like ordinary care.

Approach at a Glance

Unit of Analysis

Bar for the List

Our Methodology

We don't start by scanning the entire general population for potential Part 2 sources. We start by proving the approach works against sources we already know are Part 2 — because QEs have submitted them to the designated 42 CFR endpoint. Then, once we trust the tool, we point it at the general population to find candidates that may have been missed.

Phase 1 — Calibrate Against Known 42 CFR Sources

  1. Pull a set of sample CCDs from sources that QEs have already submitted into PDR's 42 CFR-designated endpoint — these are our "known positives."
  2. Run the same code against those samples and confirm the signals light up as expected. This proves the clinical signature genuinely distinguishes treatment programs from general care, rather than flagging noise.
  3. If the code doesn't reliably flag known Part 2 sources, that tells us our indicators or thresholds need adjustment — we iterate until it does a good job.
A grain of salt on the "known" set. This reference set is imperfect. Some organizations are highly conservative and route entirely ordinary, non-SUD data to the Part 2 endpoint out of an abundance of caution. So a source sitting in the Part 2 location does not, by itself, prove its data carries a strong SUD signature. We treat the known set as a useful guide, not as ground truth — looking for the clinical signature in the data rather than assuming the routing was always correct.

Phase 2 — Sanity Check on General Population

  1. Run the same code against a sample of sources from the general (non-42-CFR) pipeline.
  2. Confirm that ordinary general-care sources score well below the candidate threshold — this guards against a tool that's so loose it flags everything.
  3. If too many general sources score as candidates, we tighten the approach before generating the real candidate list.

Phase 3 — Candidate Identification Run

  1. Only once Phases 1 and 2 give us confidence that the tool works both ways (finds known positives, doesn't flag obvious negatives) do we run the broader candidate scan.
  2. Sources whose pattern is consistent with SUD treatment activity become candidates.

Phase 4 — Craft Letters to the QEs

  1. For each candidate source, generate a write-up addressed to the responsible QE.
  2. The letter lays out our methodology — what we looked for, how we scored it, why we calibrated against known 42 CFR sources first — and then presents the specific evidence for why this particular source warrants investigation.
  3. The tone is professional and neutral: here's what we found, here's why it's worth a conversation, please research and let us know.

Phase 5 — QE Research and Remediation

  1. QEs receive the letters and contact the flagged sources to determine whether they operate as (or contain) a Part 2 program.
  2. Based on their findings, QEs make changes to submission routing as appropriate — either confirming the source is fine as-is, or rerouting it to the 42 CFR pipeline.
  3. QEs notify the PDR team of any retroactive changes needed for data already in the general bucket.
Why this order matters: if we went straight to scanning the general population without calibrating first, we'd have no way to know whether our results are meaningful. By proving the tool works on known cases first, we can stand behind the candidate list with confidence — and explain to each QE exactly what methodology produced their source's inclusion.

Cases We Are Accounting For

  • A treatment unit inside a general facility: a hospital can have a dedicated detox or addiction medicine unit that behaves like a Part 2 program even though the hospital as a whole looks like ordinary acute care. We look at the specific care location, not just the source overall, so this doesn't get averaged away.
  • Behavioral health vs. substance use: a facility with a lot of general psychiatric content but little substance-use content isn't a Part 2 signal — we're specifically looking for substance use disorder patterns, not mental health broadly.
  • Routine office-based treatment: a primary care practice where a physician occasionally prescribes buprenorphine isn't automatically a Part 2 program. We weigh that signal lightly and look for stronger corroborating evidence before it affects a source's candidacy.
  • Facility name isn't the whole story: an obviously-named recovery center showing a strong pattern is a confirmation, not a discovery — we're most interested in sources with a generic or unrelated name showing the same pattern, since that's the case most likely to have been missed.
  • Patient history vs. this visit: a patient telling a provider about past or outside substance use treatment doesn't make that provider a Part 2 program. We only count activity tied to care actually delivered at that visit.

What Happens With the Candidate List

Result What Happens
Source or location shows a strong SUD pattern The QE receives a write-up and is asked to research the source and report back on whether it operates as, or contains, a Part 2 program.
QE confirms it IS Part 2 but data was submitted as standard The QE engages their privacy and security team so the PDR and QE can build a specific remediation plan together — Part 2-protected data has been flowing through the standard pipeline.
QE researches and determines it is not a Part 2 program Closed out — no further action. This is an expected and acceptable outcome, not a failure of the approach.
Source shows only a weak or borderline pattern Still included, but flagged as lower priority — worth a lighter check rather than an urgent one.
This tool does not move data or change access controls on its own. It builds a research list. The QE's research and any resulting routing change are separate, deliberate steps that happen after this process.
